\section{Overview: Measuring Representation, from Ballots to Language}
% ===============================================================

Societies increasingly make collective decisions through algorithms. Cities
allocate budgets with voting rules, parliaments are elected under competing
electoral systems, and large language models now summarize public
consultations and draft consensus statements. Each of these raises the same
question: \emph{does the outcome faithfully represent the people it claims to
represent?} Answering it requires a science of \emph{measurement} for
collective decisions: principled ways to compare decision problems, to
quantify the structure of preferences, and to evaluate outcomes against real
data. Until recently, computational social choice had few such tools: it was
a largely theoretical field, with scarce real data and experiments run on
votes drawn uniformly at random. My work has changed this. I introduced the
measurement tools, built the datasets, and helped set the experimental
standards that the field now uses. At EPFL, I will take the next step:
\textbf{measuring and guaranteeing representation as collective decisions
move from ballots to language.}

I bring three assets to this program. I built each of them, and each is now
used by others:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{A geometry of collective decisions.} I introduced \emph{maps of
  elections}, based on distances that measure how similar two decision
  problems are, independently of who the candidates and voters are. The
  founding paper has over 60 citations, the line of work has been cited in
  more than 120 papers, over 50 of them by researchers outside my circle of
  coauthors, and reviewers now ask authors to use diverse data ``in the times
  of the map of elections.''
  \item \emph{Real data at scale.} I initiated \emph{Pabulib}, the largest
  open repository of participatory budgeting data (2{,}053 elections from 30
  cities in 8 countries, 8.6 million votes). Nearly all experimental research
  on participatory budgeting now uses it, and its analyses helped convince
  cities in Poland and across Europe to adopt the Method of Equal Shares.
  \item \emph{A channel to practice.} I co-founded \emph{Science for
  Democracy}, a nonprofit that helps municipalities adopt proportional voting
  rules.
\end{itemize}
I have led this agenda from its start. As a PhD student, I was the principal
investigator of a National Science Centre grant (NCN Preludium) on the
\emph{election isomorphism problem}, which grew into maps of elections. The
positionwise distance at the core of the framework was my own idea, I
designed and implemented all the experiments and software behind it, and I am
lead author of its central papers\venue{AAMAS'20, IJCAI'22, AIJ'25}. My thesis,
the first in the field to take the whole space of elections as its object of
study, was defended with distinction at AGH University in 2024 and received
the Polish Prime Minister's Award (2025); my doctoral work also earned a START
fellowship of the Foundation for Polish Science (2023). Since then, a CNRS
postdoc at LAMSADE (Paris Dauphine-PSL) and my position at the University of
Geneva have taken this program into the domain of language.

\section{Past and Current Research}
% ===============================================================

\subsection{How similar are two elections? A geometry of collective decisions}
Two elections may have entirely different candidates (presidential candidates
vs.\ types of sushi), so a meaningful distance between them must be invariant
to renaming candidates and reordering voters. In my NCN-funded project, we
showed that the most natural such \emph{isomorphic distances}, such as the
number of adjacent swaps needed to make two elections isomorphic, are NP-hard
to compute, hard to approximate, and hard in the parameterized sense; even
CPLEX and Gurobi fail on them\venue{AAAI'19; JCSS'25; AAAI'22; JAIR'24}. My
answer was the \emph{positionwise distance}: represent each election by its
positional matrix (how often each candidate takes each rank) and compute the
optimal matching of candidates under the $\ell_1$ norm. It runs in polynomial
time and is highly accurate in practice, and it let me draw the first \emph{map
of elections}: compute pairwise distances among a collection of elections and
embed them in the plane so that similar elections lie close to each
other\venue{AAMAS'20}. We then gave the map interpretable reference
points\venue{IJCAI'21}, extended it to approval elections, in a paper on
statistical cultures for approval voting that the community now treats as the
reference\venue{IJCAI'22}, to elections of different sizes through
\emph{feature distances}\venue{AAAI'25}, and to preference learning: through
the geometry of expected frequency matrices, we learn the parameters of the
Mallows model in polynomial time, where earlier Kemeny-based approaches were
NP-hard\venue{NeurIPS'22}. The full framework appeared in \emph{Artificial
Intelligence}\venue{AIJ'25}.

Maps turned out to be a methodology, not just a picture, and they corrected
how the field ran experiments. They revealed that the Mallows model, the most
popular model of random votes, had been used for years without normalizing its
dispersion parameter by the number of candidates, so that its instances
crowded into a narrow region of the space; the normalization I proposed is now
standard. They showed that the running time of ILP solvers for NP-hard rules
grows with an election's distance from impartial culture, and that a family of
group-separable elections that no earlier paper on Dodgson's rule had
considered is especially hard for it. They also showed how little of the space the available real-life elections
covered, which motivated others to collect more: Boehmer and Schaar
(AAMAS'23) added nearly 8{,}000 instances to PrefLib so that they could be
placed on the map. I extended the framework to stable roommates and stable
matching instances, in a paper with two fellow PhD students and no senior
coauthor\venue{AAMAS'23 \emph{Best Student Paper Award}; JAIR'24}, and later
to tournaments, fair division\venue{AAAI'26}, and voting rules themselves. To
make the methodology standard practice, I co-authored a community
guide\venue{IJCAI'24}, gave tutorials at AAAI, AAMAS, and IJCAI, and built two open-source
Python libraries: \texttt{mapel}, for drawing maps, and
\texttt{prefsampling}, for sampling synthetic preferences.

\subsection{Discovering structure in preferences}
Beyond comparing elections, I develop measures of what is \emph{inside} them.
Polarization has long been studied in political elections where voters name
one party, but was almost entirely neglected for ordinal and approval ballots.
We introduced indices of agreement, diversity, and polarization built on a
single generalization of Kemeny's rule, \emph{$k$-Kemeny}, which seeks $k$
representative rankings instead of one consensus; the problem is NP-hard, and
we gave efficient approximation algorithms and extended the indices to
approval elections\venue{IJCAI'23; IJCAI'26}, then used them to quantify how
diverse structured preference domains are\venue{AAAI'26; AAMAS'26}. During my
postdoc with J\'er\^ome Lang, I initiated and led a line of work that inverts
the usual direction of the field: instead of designing a rule, we
\emph{discover hidden properties of a preference dataset}, such as clones,
antagonistic candidates, and diverse groups of candidates\venue{AAMAS'24;
IJCAI'24}, and, in ongoing work, hidden structure among voters, such as
clusters of similar voters and outliers. This brings social choice close to
the central task of machine learning: finding structure in data. Closer to
political science, I designed voting rules that let
allied candidates avoid the spoiler effect\venue{AAMAS'24} and a
nonparametric test for gerrymandering\venue{IJCAI'24}.

\subsection{Real data and real deployments}
In participatory budgeting (PB), residents vote on how to spend part of a
city's budget. In 2020 I initiated \emph{Pabulib}, with D.~Stolicki and
N.~Talmon, and I have led its development since\venue{IJCAI'23}. It now holds
2{,}053 elections from 30 cities in 8 countries, with 8.6 million votes on
over 38{,}000 projects; researchers abroad now persuade their own cities to
contribute, which is how Amsterdam and Budapest entered it. Pabulib is the
standard dataset for PB research and, increasingly, for approval voting in
general: more than 70 papers have used it, including field studies of
participatory processes in Switzerland and Taiwan. Real data opened questions
that theory alone could not answer: learning realistic models of approval
voting\venue{AAMAS'25}, measuring how well individual projects performed under
a given rule\venue{IJCAI'24}, and a game-theoretic analysis of how proposers
set project costs under the Method of Equal Shares\venue{NeurIPS'25}.

This work reaches practice. The analyses in the Pabulib paper were used by the
team, of which I am a member, that convinced cities in Poland and across Europe
to adopt the Method of Equal Shares, starting with Wieliczka, the first city to
use it. Proportional rules sometimes fund a costly project over a cheaper and
more popular one, which is correct but surprising to residents, so I have also
worked on ways of presenting outcomes that explain such decisions. In 2025 I
co-founded \emph{Nauka dla Demokracji} (Science for Democracy), a nonprofit
that connects researchers with the officials who run democratic processes.
Pabulib now operates under its auspices, closing the loop between the data
that cities produce and the research that improves their processes.

\subsection{Toward language}
At CNRS LAMSADE, I built simulation frameworks for multi-agent
decision-making. At the University of Geneva, I build NLP pipelines that
extract structured information from treaties, diplomatic communications, and
policy documents, and I model decision-making dynamics in multilateral
negotiations. Bringing these together, in \emph{VoteSim}\venue{EMNLP'26} we
simulate democratic deliberation among LLM agents and show how the choice of
electoral system shapes whether pluralistic preferences are represented.

\section{Research Agenda}
% ===============================================================

Collective decisions are moving from ballots to language. Residents submit
free-text proposals, consultations collect thousands of comments, and AI
systems summarize, mediate, and increasingly vote on people's behalf. Recent
work has made a promising start. \emph{Generative social choice}
(Fish et al., EC'24) gives proportional-representation guarantees for slates
of LLM-generated statements, and the \emph{Habermas Machine} (Tessler et al.,
\emph{Science} 2024) showed that LLM-mediated deliberation can find common
ground at scale. Three things are still missing: ways to \emph{measure and
audit} representation in text, an understanding of the \emph{dynamics} that
arise when AI agents take part repeatedly, and \emph{validation on real data}
rather than synthetic opinions. These are exactly the gaps that my work
closed for ballots. My agenda has three connected thrusts;
each poses concrete open problems and has a natural first PhD project.

\thrust{Thrust A: Representation guarantees for collective decisions in language.}
\begin{itemize}
  \problem{Auditing representation in text.} Given individual opinions
  written in natural language and an aggregate text (a summary or a consensus
  statement), how should we measure how proportionally the aggregate
  represents the population? Which measures satisfy natural axioms, and which
  can be estimated with few LLM queries and provable error bounds? Existing
  methods \emph{generate} representative text; we lack principled ways to
  \emph{evaluate} it. My structure-discovery methods, which find clusters and
  outliers among voters, are the natural starting point for finding the groups
  that an aggregate text leaves out.
  \problem{Polarization dynamics in AI societies.} Under which electoral
  systems, discussion structures, and mixes of model families does repeated
  deliberation among LLM agents converge, fragment, or polarize? I conjecture
  that majoritarian systems accelerate polarization and that diversity of
  model families slows it. My polarization indices provide the measures, and
  records of real multilateral negotiations provide a human baseline.
  \problem{Faithful AI proxies, tested on real ballots.} Pabulib contains real
  approval ballots over projects with textual descriptions, which is rare
  ground truth for AI proxies. When an LLM votes on a person's behalf, how
  much does the \emph{outcome} change under rules such as the Method of Equal
  Shares, and which rules are robust to proxy errors? This is the first PhD
  project of the thrust.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Five-year goal:} a theory of representation for text-based collective
decisions, with audit tools that practitioners can apply to AI-generated
summaries and consensus statements.

\thrust{Thrust B: Participatory democracy as a living laboratory.}
\begin{itemize}
  \problem{Richer democratic data.} I will extend Pabulib with full ballots,
  project texts, residents' comments, and outcomes over several years. This
  will turn it into a longitudinal benchmark, including for Thrust~A.
  \problem{Does the rule change the process?} Theory predicts that
  proportional rules change which projects are proposed and at what
  cost\venue{NeurIPS'25}. Field studies with partner cities can test whether
  they also change who participates and who proposes. This is the first PhD
  project of the thrust.
  \problem{Tools that cities and residents can trust.} Open tools to choose,
  simulate, and audit PB rules, and to \emph{explain} their outcomes to
  residents, so that cities can adopt evidence-based designs without in-house
  experts and residents can see why a project was or was not funded.
\end{itemize}
Switzerland is an ideal setting for this work, with its tradition of direct
democracy, Lausanne's participatory budget, and Swiss cities such as Aarau
and Winterthur that have already used the Method of Equal
Shares. % TODO: verify Aarau/Winterthur
I will partner with them as I do through Science for Democracy.

\thrust{Thrust C: A geometry of collective decision problems, from ballots to text.}
\begin{itemize}
  \problem{Computing invariant distances at scale.} Which domains admit
  polynomial-time relaxations of isomorphism-invariant distances that, like
  the positionwise distance, preserve the geometry? Can sketching
  and sublinear techniques make them scale to elections with millions of
  voters? This is the first PhD project of the thrust.
  \problem{Benchmarks with provable coverage.} How many instances, and which
  ones, must a benchmark contain to cover the space of instances up to a given
  precision under a given distance? Which samplers achieve this? The answers
  will become defaults in \texttt{prefsampling} and \texttt{mapel}.
  \problem{Maps of deliberation.} Extending the geometry from ballots to sets
  of textual opinions will make it possible to compare deliberations, human or
  AI, the way we now compare elections. This will give Thrust~A its evaluation
  backbone.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Students, funding, and service.} The three thrusts offer PhD projects
of different flavors: theoretical (C), empirical and NLP-oriented (A), and
applied and data-centric (B). I have already co-supervised more than ten
students, led a grant as principal investigator (NCN Preludium), and worked
within an ERC-funded project. I will apply to the Swiss National Science
Foundation early on and for an ERC Starting Grant built around Thrust~A. I
serve on the editorial board of JAIR (since 2026) and on the program
committees of NeurIPS, AAAI, AAMAS, and IJCAI.

\section{Why EPFL}
% ===============================================================

My agenda sits between algorithms, AI, and the social sciences, and EPFL is
one of the few places where all three are strong under one roof. I see
concrete joint projects:
\begin{itemize}
  \item with \emph{Michael Kapralov} on sketching and sublinear algorithms for
  isomorphism-invariant distances between very large elections (Thrust~C);
  \item with \emph{Robert West} on how faithfully LLMs represent the opinions
  of real populations, and on polarization dynamics in AI societies,
  combining his work in computational social science with my preference
  measures (Thrust~A);
  \item with \emph{Antoine Bosselut} on measuring representation in
  LLM-generated summaries and consensus statements (Thrust~A);
  \item with \emph{Bryan Ford} on secure, auditable platforms for
  participatory processes and digital deliberation (Thrust~B).
\end{itemize}
Beyond EPFL, the Lake Geneva region offers what this agenda needs most:
direct democracy, municipalities that experiment with participation, and
international organizations whose negotiations are rich records of real
deliberation.

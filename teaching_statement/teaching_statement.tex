\section{Teaching Philosophy}
% ===============================================================

In my research, I prove properties of voting rules, and I also run them on
real and synthetic data. I would like my students to do both.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Concepts come with data.} When I introduce a definition, students will
also apply it to a concrete instance. For example, students can load a real participatory
  budget from Pabulib, compute the outcomes of several voting rules, and only
  then study the axioms that explain why the outcomes differ.
  \item \emph{Open-ended problems.} Alongside standard exercises, I will set
problems without a known answer, such as finding the statistical model of
votes that best describes a city's ballots. Students then have to choose a
method themselves and argue for their conclusions.
  \item \emph{Experiments in the age of language models.} Language models now
write much of the code for experiments, so the hard part has moved to
designing an experiment and checking that the code really runs it. I will
teach students to do both, for example by asking them to find the error in a
plausible-looking experiment generated by a language model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Teaching and Mentoring Experience}
% ===============================================================

At Jagiellonian University (2018--2021), I taught \emph{Introduction to
Programming} (a full-year course), \emph{Logic and Set Theory}, and
\emph{Linear Algebra} (one semester each) to groups of about twenty
undergraduate students. Groups with very different preparation taught me to
explain one idea in several ways and to notice early who is falling behind.
For my research community, I co-authored the \emph{Guide to Numerical
Experiments on Elections}\pub{C15}, developed \texttt{mapof}, co-developed
\texttt{prefsampling}, and presented the tutorial \emph{Experiments in Social
Choice} at IJCAI 2023, AAMAS 2023, and AAAI 2024. In 2026, I gave a hands-on
workshop for newcomers to social choice at the University of Chicago's Data
Science Institute.
I have also given more than thirty research talks, which has made me
comfortable explaining technical material to mixed audiences.

I have co-supervised more than ten BSc and MSc students, and several of
these projects led to papers at AAAI 2025\pub{C19} and ECAI
2025\pub{C22}.
I start each student with a well-defined question for which data and tools
are at hand, so that a first result comes quickly, and then move to an open
problem whose answer I do not know.
As President of the PhD Students' Association at Jagiellonian University, I
led an elected body representing doctoral students, which showed me how much
a student's success depends on conditions outside the classroom.

\section{Teaching at EPFL}
% ===============================================================

I am prepared to teach core bachelor courses (introductory programming,
discrete mathematics, algorithms) and master courses in artificial
intelligence and algorithmic game theory. In large bachelor courses, I would use weekly programming and problem-solving
exercises with quick feedback, automated where possible, and short problems
during lectures that show me which ideas students have not yet understood.
Exams would test both proofs and computation.
I would like to develop two new courses that complement \emph{Algorithmic
Game Theory} (MGT-435) and \emph{Technologies for Democratic Society} (CS-234):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Algorithms for Collective Decisions} (master): voting,
  committee elections, participatory budgeting, and fair division, with real data
  throughout and a final project on a real participatory budget, possibly
  with a Swiss municipality.
  \item \emph{AI and Democracy} (master seminar): AI-mediated deliberation,
  AI-written summaries of public consultations, and AI agents that vote on
  people's behalf, open to students from computer science, data science, and digital
  humanities.
\end{itemize}
I would also draw on EPFL's Teaching Support Center and mid-semester
student feedback.

\section{Teaching Philosophy: Learning by Measuring}
% ===============================================================

The most useful thing I learned during my PhD, I had to learn on my own.
My advisor's work was theoretical, and when I wanted to know how voting rules
actually behave, nobody around me could tell me how to design a numerical
experiment, which data to use, or how to tell a real finding from an artifact
of the generator. Working this out became my thesis, and then a tutorial, a
community guide, and two software libraries, so that others would not have to
start from scratch. This experience shapes how I teach. I want students to
\emph{measure} the objects they study, not only to prove things about them,
because a student who has computed a Kemeny ranking on a real election and
watched the ILP solver stall on a specific kind of profile understands
NP-hardness in a way that no reduction on a blackboard can give.

In practice, this means three things in my courses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Every abstraction comes with an instance.} I introduce a
  definition together with a dataset or a generator on which students can
  try it in the same session. In my field this is easy: a city's
  participatory budget is a real election with real stakes, and students can
  load one from Pabulib, run three voting rules on it, and argue about which
  outcome is fairer before they have seen a single axiom.
  \item \emph{Students should discover, not only verify.} The exercises I
  like best have an answer that I do not know in advance. ``Which statistical
  culture best approximates the preferences of Warsaw's residents?'' is a
  question a second-year student can attack with the tools from my research,
  and the answers are sometimes publishable.
  \item \emph{Tools are part of the curriculum.} Writing clean, reproducible
  experimental code is a skill that most students are never taught. I teach
  it explicitly, using \texttt{prefsampling} and \texttt{mapel} as both
  examples and infrastructure, and I ask students to contribute to them
  instead of writing throwaway scripts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Teaching Experience}
% ===============================================================

As a Teaching and Research Assistant at Jagiellonian University (2018--2021),
I taught the exercise sessions of three core bachelor courses in computer
science: \emph{Introduction to Programming}, \emph{Logic and Set Theory}, and
\emph{Linear Algebra}. These are first- and second-year courses with large
cohorts and a wide spread of preparation, and they taught me to explain the
same idea in several ways and to notice early which students are falling
behind. % TODO: add cohort sizes, evaluation scores, or any teaching award if available
They also cover the material I would most like to teach at the bachelor
level at EPFL: programming, discrete mathematics, and algorithms are the
foundation on which everything in my research stands.

Beyond university courses, I have taught my research community. I gave a
tutorial on numerical experiments in computational social choice at three of
the major AI conferences, AAAI, AAMAS, and IJCAI, % TODO: add years
and I co-authored the \emph{Guide to Numerical Experiments on Elections}
(IJCAI-24), which is written as teaching material: it surveys how experiments
have been done, explains what goes wrong, and gives concrete recommendations.
The two open-source libraries I maintain, \texttt{prefsampling} and
\texttt{mapel}, are used by other groups both for research and in student
projects. I have also given more than twenty talks at conferences and
seminars, and explaining voting rules to city officials and residents through
\emph{Science for Democracy} has been the most demanding teaching I have done:
the audience has no patience for notation and every question is about a real
decision.

\section{Mentoring and Supervision}
% ===============================================================

I have co-supervised more than ten bachelor's and master's students, mostly at
AGH University of Kraków, within an ERC-funded project, and at LAMSADE in
Paris. Several of these projects grew into publications at AAAI, AAMAS, ECAI,
and NeurIPS with the student as a coauthor. % TODO: name 2--3 students and papers (e.g. Nikolow ECAI'25, Mertlová/Nunn AAAI'25, Szwagierczak NeurIPS'25) and say where they are now
My approach follows the way I was mentored and the way I would have liked to
be:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Start small and concrete.} A student's first project is a
  well-defined experimental question with a dataset and a tool already in
  hand, so that the first result comes within weeks. Confidence comes from a
  finished thing, however small.
  \item \emph{Then hand over an open problem.} Once a student has shipped
  something, I give them a question whose answer I do not know, and I expect
  them to disagree with me. The best paper award I received at AAMAS-23 was
  for a paper written by three PhD students without a senior coauthor, and I
  want my own students to have that experience.
  \item \emph{Teach the craft, not only the result.} I review students' code
  and experimental design as carefully as their proofs, and I ask them to
  present their work to the group early and often. Writing clearly and
  presenting well are skills I teach deliberately, because they decide
  whether good work is noticed.
\end{itemize}
As President of the PhD Students' Association at Jagiellonian University and
as a representative in the Polish national association of doctoral
candidates, I spent two years working on the conditions under which doctoral
students work: funding, supervision rules, and representation in university
bodies. I know from that work how much a student's success depends on things
outside the research itself, and I will pay attention to them in my group.

\section{Teaching at EPFL}
% ===============================================================

\textbf{Existing courses.} I am prepared to teach core courses at the
bachelor level, in particular introductory programming, discrete mathematics,
algorithms, and theory of computation, and at the master level, artificial
intelligence and algorithmic game theory. I speak French at a conversational
level and am improving it actively; I expect to teach bachelor courses in
French within my first years at EPFL. % TODO: adjust to what you are comfortable committing to

\textbf{New courses.} I would like to develop two courses that do not yet
exist in most computer science curricula:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Algorithms for Collective Decisions} (master level). Voting,
  participatory budgeting, fair division, and matching, taught through the
  lens of my research: every rule comes with real data, every axiom with an
  experiment, and the project is a full study of a real participatory
  process, from data to recommendation. The course would draw students from
  computer science, data science, and the social sciences.
  \item \emph{AI and Democracy} (master seminar). How language models change
  collective decision-making: AI-mediated deliberation, LLM proxies,
  representation in generated text, and the risks of each. The seminar would
  combine recent papers with hands-on simulation, and it would be a natural
  meeting point for students of the School of Computer and Communication
  Sciences and of the humanities and social sciences at EPFL.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Beyond the classroom.} Through Science for Democracy I have learned
how to explain algorithms to people who will be governed by them, and I would
bring this to EPFL: student projects with Swiss municipalities that run
participatory budgets, public explanations of how their votes are counted,
and open tools that residents can use. Teaching people why a proportional
rule funded one project and not another is, to me, the same job as teaching
students why an algorithm is correct.

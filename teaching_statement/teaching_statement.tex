\section{Teaching Philosophy}
% ===============================================================

My research combines two ways of understanding collective decisions: proving
what voting rules guarantee, and measuring how they behave on real and
synthetic data. I want my students to learn both, and to see how each informs
the other. A theorem tells them what a rule guarantees; an experiment shows
them how often that guarantee matters in practice. Three principles follow.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Every concept is paired with data.} When I introduce a
  definition, students also apply it to a concrete instance. In collective
  decision making this is natural: students can load a real participatory
  budget from Pabulib, compute the outcomes of several voting rules, and only
  then study the axioms that explain why the outcomes differ.
  \item \emph{Students learn to ask questions, not only to answer them.}
  Alongside standard exercises, I will set open-ended problems, such as
  finding the statistical model of votes that best describes a city's
  ballots. Such problems have no answer in the textbook: students must design
  a method, test it, and defend their conclusions.
  \item \emph{Computational practice is taught explicitly.} Writing clean,
  reproducible experimental code is a core skill for computer scientists, yet
  it is rarely taught as such. I will teach it with \texttt{prefsampling} and
  \texttt{mapof}, the Python libraries I released, so that students work with
  research-grade tools from the start.
\end{itemize}

\section{Teaching and Mentoring Experience}
% ===============================================================

At Jagiellonian University (2018--2021), I taught \emph{Introduction to
Programming} (a full-year course), \emph{Logic and Set Theory}, and
\emph{Linear Algebra} (one semester each) to groups of about fifteen
undergraduate students.
For my research community, I co-authored the \emph{Guide to Numerical
Experiments on Elections}\pub{C15} and released \texttt{prefsampling} and
\texttt{mapof}, and I co-presented the tutorial \emph{Experiments in Social
Choice} at IJCAI 2023, AAMAS 2023, and AAAI 2024.
I have given more than thirty talks.

I have co-supervised more than ten BSc and MSc students, and work with
students led to conference papers at AAAI 2025\pub{C19} and ECAI
2025\pub{C22}.
I start each student with a well-defined question for which data and tools
are at hand, so that a first result comes quickly, and then move to an open
problem whose answer I do not know. I review experimental design, code, and
writing as carefully as proofs.
As President of the PhD Students' Association at Jagiellonian University, I
led an elected body representing doctoral students.

\section{Teaching at EPFL}
% ===============================================================

I am prepared to teach core bachelor courses (introductory programming,
discrete mathematics, algorithms) and master courses in artificial
intelligence and algorithmic game theory. In large bachelor courses, I would
apply the same principles at scale: weekly programming and problem-solving
exercises with quick feedback, automated where possible, and short problems
during lectures that show me which ideas students have not yet understood.
Exams would test both proofs and computation.
I would also like to develop two new courses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Algorithms for Collective Decisions} (master): voting,
  participatory budgeting, fair division, and matching, with real data
  throughout and a final project on a real participatory budget.
  \item \emph{AI and Democracy} (master seminar): AI-mediated deliberation,
  AI-written summaries of public consultations, and AI agents that vote on
  people's behalf, open to students from computer science and the social
  sciences.
\end{itemize}
Beyond the classroom, I would like to offer student projects with Swiss
municipalities that run participatory budgets.
